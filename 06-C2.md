# Level C2: Mastery

**Prerequisite:** Milestone C1 reached (step C1-353 ticked). The README methods still apply; the weekly rhythm continues in the C2 form below.

**Goal at exit:** you use Danish like an educated native speaker who reads. You can follow anything spoken in any standard or regional variety at any speed, including revue wordplay, parliamentary debate and 1940s film dialogue; read any Danish text from the medieval ballads and Holberg to this year's debut novels, with the history of the language in your ear; write in any genre, including literary prose, satire, the formal speech and the academic article, at a level an editor would print with light changes; speak for twenty minutes without notes on a specialised subject, moderate a debate, give a wedding speech, and switch register and dialect features for effect; translate between English and Danish in both directions; and proofread Danish to publication standard. Vocabulary above 18,000 words, including the older and regional vocabulary needed for the canon. There is no Danish test above Studieprøven; this level ends with a portfolio that stands in for one.

**Time:** about 630 hours over 36 weeks, roughly 3 hours a day, 6 days a week. At 2 hours a day the level takes about 55 weeks; keep the order.

**What changes at C2** (Appendix A): the canon is read from its beginning: Brandes, Holberg, Jacobsen, Pontoppidan's *Lykke-Per* in full, Johannes V. Jensen, Martin A. Hansen, Inger Christensen complete, Andersen for adults in his own spelling, Kierkegaard, Villy Sørensen, Højholt, and two contemporary novels. Language study becomes linguistics, language history, rhetoric, translation, proofreading and pastiche. Listening reaches the archive: DR Bonanza, radio drama, 1940s film, revues, Folketing debates. Speaking becomes performance: the lecture, the speech, the podcast. Writing becomes literature.

## The C2 weekly rhythm

- **R1 Listening** (about 240 min): *Genstart* two episodes; *Deadline* two; two episodes from the *Klog på sprog* archive (working through it from the beginning; each summarised in three sentences, one new term to Anki); one Folketing debate segment of 30 minutes on ft.dk (the weekly *spørgetime* or a *forespørgselsdebat*), followed by reading the same passage in Folketingstidende and noting five differences between what was said and what was transcribed; *Sprogminuttet* (P1's daily language minute) every day; one long programme in rotation (*Magten*, *Skønlitteratur på P1*, *Brinkmanns briks*, *Kampen om historien*, *Fuld af viden*, *Supertanker*) summarised in eight sentences; every second week a radio drama (a *DR Radiodrama* production or a *Radioklassikeren* episode on DR Lyd, about an hour) instead of the long programme.
- **R2 Speaking** (about 150 min): four AI sessions of 20 minutes in rotation (seminar; debate moderation with the assistant playing two guests; an interview in which you are the expert; small talk in casual register with slang); one recorded performance a week alternating between a 12-minute *foredrag* without notes (odd weeks) and a 5-minute *tale* for an occasion or a 10-minute podcast segment (even weeks), transcribed and edited; five minutes a day of shadowing a DR newsreader or a *Deadline* host for prosody; one 3-minute dictation from a radio drama or a Weekendavisen audio article.
- **R3 Writing and reading** (about 220 min): *læsedagbog* five days, now 150 words with a stylistic observation and one sentence imitated; one writing-bank text (Appendix I) of 600 words, edited and filed in `writing/portfolio-C2.md`; Weekendavisen read in full (every section) across the week; the daily paper, two articles a day; one academic article from tidsskrift.dk or one literary-magazine essay a week; ten harvested cards (older words from the canon, collocations, revue expressions).
- **R4 Poem or song** (about 40 min): one item from Appendix J by heart, performed on the weekly recording.

**Book pattern** (C2 form): (1) before: lex.dk, DLH, and one scholarly introduction or afterword (most Danske Klassikere and Gyldendal editions have one); (2) during: older texts read with ODS open; audiobook where one exists; extensive with at most 5 lookups per 30 pages, marking sentences to imitate; (3) after: a 600-word essay (not a school analysis: an argued reading with a thesis), an editor pass, a 10-minute seminar with the assistant playing a literature lecturer, and five imitated sentences.

**Archive pattern** (Huset på Christianshavn, films of the 1940s–1960s): no subtitles; note ten words or expressions that have changed or gone since; look each up in ODS or DDO with its date.

## Appendix A: What changes at C2

1. **The canon from the beginning.** Ballads, *Jyske Lov*, Holberg, Brandes, Jacobsen, Pontoppidan in full, Jensen, Hansen, Christensen, Andersen and Kierkegaard in their own spelling, and then the present. Every book gets a scholarly introduction, an essay with a thesis, and a seminar. By the end you can read any Danish text since 1500 without a modernised edition.
2. **Language study is linguistics and craft.** *Grammatik over det Danske Sprog* for the system, the language council's archive for norms and change, the historical dictionaries for older text, the speech archive for rhetoric, and then translation, proofreading and pastiche as tests of control.
3. **The archive.** *Huset på Christianshavn*, radio drama, 1940s–60s film and Dirch Passer give you the Danish of the twentieth century as it was spoken; the revues give you wordplay at its fastest; Folketinget gives you public speech at length, with the transcript to check against.
4. **Performance.** Twelve-minute lectures without notes, occasional speeches in the Danish tradition (the *tale* is a genre Danes are judged on), podcast segments, moderation, pastiche read aloud. Fluency is assumed; the target is command.
5. **Literature as output.** The *novelle*, the essay in the tradition of Villy Sørensen and Suzanne Brøgger, the revue monologue, the kronik on language. The editor instruction's verdict "publishable with light edits" is the standard.
6. **Translation both ways** against printed translations. Nothing shows the gap between languages as exactly.
7. **The neighbours in the original.** A Norwegian and a Swedish novel, read without a dictionary. Receptive command of the three Scandinavian languages is part of what an educated Dane has.
8. **The stx exam.** The written Danish exam that Danish eighteen-year-olds sit is the nearest thing to a C2 test; two full sets under time (real sets where you can get them, otherwise sets built to the ministry's published specification), marked by the ministry's own grade descriptions, at a grade a Dane would be pleased with.
9. **Portfolio, not certificate.** No institution issues a C2 in Danish. The exit check is designed so that every claim in the CEFR C2 descriptors is backed by a recording or a document you could show.
10. **Afterwards.** Ten cards a day, the reading diary, one book a month, and the habit of noticing. The level does not end; it becomes how you live in the language.

## Appendix B: C2 language checklist

1. **Information structure**: front-field choice explained for every sentence; focus and theme; heavy constituents; the *sætningsknude*; the *der*-construction's exact conditions.
2. **Modal particles**: all fourteen in their GDS senses, combined (*jo nok, da vel, nu nok, skam ikke*), with the right stress and intonation.
3. **Determination in depth**: generic and specific reference, bare nouns, definiteness with modifiers, proper-name usage, the definite suffix versus the article for emphasis.
4. **Complementation**: which verbs take *at*-clause, *om*-clause, infinitive, bare infinitive, *hv*-clause, and with what meaning difference.
5. **Aspect and tense in narrative art**: the historical present, free indirect style (*dækket direkte tale*) and its tense logic, the pluperfect as memory.
6. **Passives and impersonals in every register**, including the archaic (*vorder*), the administrative and the literary.
7. **Older morphology, receptive**: plural verb forms, *-e* infinitives, the genitive in *-s* on phrases, *thi, hvilken, saaledes, tilforn*, pre-1948 orthography read at sight, eighteenth-century orthography with a crib.
8. **Language history**: the periods, the sound changes, the 1948 reform, the 2024 changes, and the ability to date a text by its language within twenty years.
9. **Dialect and sociolect**: features of each main dialect, Copenhagen high and low, *multietnolekt*, and the ability to imitate two of them recognisably.
10. **Neighbouring languages**: Norwegian and Swedish read as novels; Nynorsk and older Swedish recognised.
11. **Rhetoric**: the classical apparatus in Danish terms; the conventions of every Danish occasional speech; moderation; the New Year address; the revue monologue.
12. **Humour**: pun mechanisms, Storm P.'s logic, ATS conventions, the *underdrivelse* as a national register, revue satire.
13. **Register at will**, including deliberate breach for effect, archaism for irony, and the *kancellistil* as parody.
14. **Genre command**: novelle, essay, kronik, leder, anmeldelse, nekrolog, tale, portræt, reportage, høringssvar, notat, abstract, subtitle, translator's note.
15. **Style analysis and pastiche**: the ability to name an author from a page and to write a page that names itself.
16. **Translation**: fidelity, idiom, rhythm; subtitle norms; the systematic asymmetries between Danish and English (compression, compounds, particles, tense).
17. **Proofreading**: the marks, the rules, the eye; 40 errors in 1500 words caught with at most one miss.
18. **Editing**: structure, cutting, sharpening a thesis, killing nominalisation, rhythm.
19. **Idiom and quotation**: 300 idioms; the *bevingede ord* Danes quote (Grundtvig, Kierkegaard, Storm P., PH, Matador, Olsen-banden); proverbs.
20. **Older and regional vocabulary**: 500 items from the canon with their ODS senses; 200 dialect items.
21. **Prosody**: newsreader, lecturer, conversational and revue deliveries distinguishable on tape; stød and stress native-like in every register.
22. **Discourse in speech**: moderation phrases, turn management in a three-way debate, the art of the *indskudt bemærkning*, backchannels in the right dialect.
23. **Names of things, complete**: institutions, offices, laws, historical events, literary periods, the canon's titles and dates.
24. **Self-editing**: every text two passes; every recording listened to once; the repair list maintained.
25. **Judgement**: knowing which Danish to use for which Dane, and when to say less.

## Appendix E: C2 exit reading test

**Sproget som arv**

Der er en bestemt slags dansk, som ingen længere taler, men som alle, der har gået i skole her, bærer rundt på. Det er det sprog, der ligger i salmebogen, i *Jyske Lov*s fortale, i Grundtvigs sange, i Holbergs komedier og i de replikker fra *Matador*, som man kan høre citeret ved et hvilket som helst middagsselskab, hvis man venter længe nok. Det er ikke et sprog, man lærer; det er et sprog, man arver, og som de fleste arvinger er vi ikke særlig opmærksomme på, hvad vi har fået.

Det opdager man først, når nogen mangler det. Jeg havde for nogle år siden en kollega, som var kommet til landet som voksen og talte et dansk, der i enhver målbar henseende var bedre end mit: mere korrekt, mere præcist, renset for de småfejl og dialektrester, som jeg selv slæber rundt på. Alligevel var der noget, der ikke var der. Når nogen ved frokostbordet sagde "det var så lidt", og en anden svarede "ja, det var det", og alle grinede, forstod han ordene, men ikke latteren. Han vidste ikke, at man i det øjeblik citerede en replik, som hele bordet havde hørt som børn, og at latteren ikke gjaldt vitsen, men genkendelsen.

Man kunne kalde det en uretfærdighed, og det er det på en måde også. Sproget som arv er en form for kapital, der uddeles efter fødsel og skolegang og ikke efter fortjeneste. Men det er samtidig den eneste kapital, der bliver større af at blive delt. Hver gang min kollega spurgte, hvad der var så morsomt, og fik svaret, blev han en smule rigere, og det gjorde vi andre også, for det er umuligt at forklare, hvorfor "det var så lidt" er sjovt, uden at komme til at tænke over, hvad det egentlig er, vi har fået overleveret, og af hvem.

Det er her, litteraturhistorien får en betydning, som den sjældent tillægges. Man læser ikke Holberg for at få at vide, hvad der skete i 1723, og heller ikke, med fuld respekt, fordi *Erasmus Montanus* er verdens bedste komedie. Man læser ham, fordi den måde, hvorpå danskere i tre hundrede år har grinet ad den, der har læst for meget og forstået for lidt, stadig er den måde, vi griner på. Per Degn lever i enhver folketingsdebat. Jeppe lever i enhver diskussion om, hvem der skal have skylden. Det er ikke litteraturhistorie; det er en brugsanvisning til det samfund, man lever i.

Jeg vil derfor tillade mig at foreslå noget, som lyder gammeldags: at den, der for alvor vil lære dansk, ikke skal nøjes med det dansk, der tales, men også skal tilegne sig det dansk, der citeres. Det er et større arbejde, og det er ikke det, sprogskolerne har tid til. Men det er den eneste vej ind i latteren ved frokostbordet, og latteren er, når alt kommer til alt, den mest pålidelige prøve på, om man er kommet hjem i et sprog.

Min kollega bestod den prøve efter nogle år. Det skete en dag, hvor en af os kom for sent til et møde og undskyldte sig med, at toget havde været forsinket, og han, uden at se op fra sine papirer, sagde: "Ja, ja, Larsen, det siger De hver gang." Vi grinede, og han grinede, og ingen af os behøvede at forklare noget. Det var så lidt.

Spørgsmål (svar på dansk; 7–10 kræver fortolkning):

1. Hvad forstår skribenten ved "sproget som arv"?
2. Hvordan var kollegaens dansk, målt objektivt?
3. Hvad manglede kollegaen ved frokostbordet, og hvad gjaldt latteren?
4. Hvorfor kalder skribenten arven en uretfærdighed, og hvad er modargumentet?
5. Hvad er ifølge skribenten den egentlige grund til at læse Holberg?
6. Hvad foreslår skribenten, at den, der vil lære dansk, skal gøre ud over at lære det talte sprog?
7. Hvad er tekstens genre, og hvilke træk viser det?
