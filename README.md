# Danish Self-Study Roadmap: Zero to C2

A literal, sequential, checkbox-driven guide for learning Danish entirely by self-study, with no tutor and no human speaking partner. There is one path. Every day you do your two dailies (Anki and the dagbog), then the next unticked step. Each step is one activity in one sitting, and it says what "finished" means. You never have to decide what to do next or how to fit things together. Do not skip ahead.

## How to read a step

Every step looks like this:

```
- [ ] A1-0070 [Study · Deep · 60 min] CD Unit 3 (a) dialogue, LRS cycle.
      Do: ...
      Done when: ...
```

- **ID** (A1-0070): level and sequence number. Steps are done strictly in ID order, one at a time.
- **One step is one sitting.** A step is a single activity that you start and finish in one go: one lesson, one episode, one chapter, one recording. Nothing in the guide runs "in the background" or "three times this week". If something has to happen four times, it is four steps, placed where they belong in the order.
- **Type tag**:
  - `Setup`: installing, buying, registering, organising.
  - `Study`: working through a lesson with a textbook or course.
  - `Drill`: memorisation and repetition (shadowing, copying, dictation, a song by heart).
  - `Input`: listening, reading, watching for understanding.
  - `Produce`: writing or speaking output (recorded monologue, AI voice session, a text).
  - `Culture`: orientation in Danish literature, film, TV and society, often in English at low levels.
  - `Check`: a verification step with a pass threshold. If you fail, the step tells you which earlier steps to repeat.
  - `Habit`: one of the two dailies. Only two steps in the whole guide have this type: the one that starts Anki and the one that starts the dagbog, both in A1.
- **Effort tag**:
  - `Casual`: relaxed. Dictionary lookups optional. You can be tired, and listening steps can be done on a walk or a commute.
  - `Focused`: normal study. Sit at a desk, no phone.
  - `Deep`: intensive study. Everything gets looked up, written down, and reviewed. Plan for the full time.
- **Time**: a realistic estimate for the one sitting.
- **Patterns.** Each level file defines a few named patterns near the top (for example the *Matador pattern* or the *podcast pattern*). A one-line step that names a pattern is done exactly as the pattern says, and it is finished when the pattern's last instruction is done.

## The six standard methods

These are referred to by name throughout the guide so they are defined once here.

### Method 1: LRS cycle (Listen, Read, Shadow)

Used for every textbook dialogue, podcast segment or short audio clip.

1. **Listen blind**: play the audio 3 times without any text. After the third time, write down in English what you think happened and any Danish words you caught.
2. **Read**: read the transcript or dialogue slowly. Look up every unknown word in Den Danske Ordbog (ordnet.dk/ddo) or the textbook glossary. Write unknown words in your notebook with the sentence they appeared in.
3. **Listen with text**: play the audio while reading along, 2 times.
4. **Shadow**: play the audio one sentence at a time. Pause, repeat the sentence aloud imitating rhythm, vowels and stød as exactly as you can. Do the whole dialogue this way twice.
5. **Shadow live**: play the audio and speak along with it simultaneously, without pausing, 3 times.
6. **Record**: record yourself reading the dialogue aloud without the audio. Listen back once. Note the 3 words you were least happy with and look them up on ordnet.dk (click the speaker icon) to hear the native pronunciation. Say each 10 times.

### Method 2: Anki card rules

- One deck named `Dansk`. Sub-decks are not needed.
- Card format: front = the Danish word **in a short phrase or sentence** (never the bare word alone), back = English meaning plus gender for nouns (`en`/`et`) and plural form, plus the past tense for verbs once you have learned it.
  - Good front: `Jeg bor i **København**.` Back: `Copenhagen (proper noun)`.
  - Good front: `et **hus**` Back: `house · huset · huse · husene`.
  - Bad front: `hus`.
- Add audio when a resource provides it. For textbook words, use ordnet.dk audio or Forvo. Do not skip words because they lack audio.
- Settings: 15 new cards per day, 200 max reviews per day, default FSRS scheduling. Never "reset" the deck.
- **Anki is a daily, non-negotiable habit** from step A1-0004 onward through C2. Missing days is the single biggest cause of failure in self-study.
- "Mature" below means a card with an interval of 21 days or more (visible in Anki Stats).

### Method 3: Recording protocol

Used for all `Produce` speaking steps.

1. Read the prompt. You get 2 minutes of silent planning. You may write 5 keywords, no more.
2. Record with your phone's voice recorder. Do not stop or restart. Silence is allowed, quitting is not.
3. Name the file `YYYY-MM-DD-<stepID>.m4a` and keep it in a `recordings` folder. You will listen to old recordings at milestones to hear your progress.
4. Listen back once. Write down every error you notice yourself. Then transcribe what you said (as accurately as you can, errors included) and run it through the correction protocol below.

### Method 4: Correction protocol (AI-assisted)

Because there is no tutor, an AI assistant (Claude, ChatGPT or similar) is your correction service. Save this prompt and reuse it every time:

```
You are a strict but kind Danish teacher. I am a [A1/A2/...] learner. Below is my Danish text.
1. List every error as a table: my version | corrected version | one-line rule.
2. Give a fully corrected version.
3. Give a version a native speaker at my level would naturally write, keeping my meaning.
4. Tell me the 2 grammar points I should review.
Do not add content I did not write.
```

Rules: read every correction and copy each corrected sentence into your notebook by hand. If the same error type appears 3 times across texts, add a card to Anki with the rule and an example.

### Method 5: Intensive vs. extensive input

- **Intensive** (`Deep` tag): short text or clip. Understand 100 percent. Every unknown word looked up and written down. Reread or replay until it is fully clear.
- **Extensive** (`Casual` tag): long text or clip. Tolerate not understanding. No pausing, no dictionary unless a word appears 3 or more times. The goal is volume and getting used to real speed.

Both are required. Intensive builds the system; extensive makes it automatic.

### Method 6: Input is the engine, and it is scheduled

Comprehensible input (Danish read, watched and heard for meaning) is the engine of this programme. Textbooks, drills and checks exist to make more of it understandable. The guide does not leave input to spare moments. It schedules it as numbered steps like everything else, and three things hold in every week of every level:

| Always true | How it appears in the steps |
|---|---|
| **A book is open.** | `Input` steps named "Reading: …", each one a sitting of a named book with named pages or chapters. Each level has a **free-reading shelf** next to the books it studies. Shelf sittings are Casual: at most 3–5 lookups, no cards, and one tracker line (title and pages). Read along with the audiobook whenever one exists. |
| **A series is running.** | `Input` steps named "Series: …", one named episode per step. When a series ends, the next one starts in the following week. One tracker line per episode. |
| **You listen for pleasure, not only to news.** | `Input` steps named "Listening: …": a named audiobook chapter you have already read, a podcast episode you have already studied, or from B1 a named documentary, radio drama or storytelling programme. They are Casual, so you can do them on a walk or a commute, but they are still done in order. |

Two more rules apply to every level:

- **Book → film.** When a book has a screen adaptation, the adaptation is a step within a week of the step that finishes the book, with Danish subtitles below C1.
- **One in two.** From B2 on, the full episode or book pattern (scene rewatches, shadowing, cards, essay) applies to every second episode or book only. The others are pure input: watch or read straight through and log one line. The step says which kind it is.

**Where to get it.** Danish public libraries give free access to **eReolen** (ereolen.dk: e-books and audiobooks), **eReolen Go** (ereolengo.dk: children's and YA books) and **Filmstriben** (filmstriben.dk: Danish films). You need a library card, which you get with MitID. Your municipality sets monthly loan limits, so reserve popular audiobooks early. Paid fallbacks: Mofibo, BookBeat or Nextory for books, and Nordisk Film+ for films. Streaming catalogues change often, so check JustWatch (Denmark) before buying or renting.

## A daily session

Every study day has the same shape:

1. **The dailies.** Clear Anki (all due cards, then the new ones). Write the dagbog entry, once the dagbog has started. These two are the only things in the guide that recur outside the numbered steps.
2. **The next step.** Open the tracker, do the first unticked step, and tick it. If you have time left, do the one after it. Never do two steps at once, and never pick a later step because it looks more convenient.

Each level file states its total hours in its `**Time:**` line and groups its steps into weeks. A "week" is a unit of work, not a calendar promise. If your days are shorter, a guide week takes you longer than seven days; keep the order and do not drop steps.

## Rules that never change

1. **Order is law.** Do steps in sequence, one at a time. A step assumes every previous step is done.
2. **Done when means done when.** Do not tick a step whose criterion you have not met.
3. **Fail a Check, go back.** Every `Check` step lists what to repeat. Repeat it, then retake the check. There is no penalty; there is also no bypass.
4. **Anki every day.** See Method 2.
5. **Speak out loud whenever a step lets you**, even at A1, even alone. Danish pronunciation is the hardest part of the language and cannot be learned silently. The steps are ordered so that hardly a day passes without one that makes you speak.
6. **Keep a paper notebook.** Grammar notes, corrected sentences, and word lists go in it by hand. Handwriting slows you down in the useful way.
7. **Track the calendar, not the clock.** Log the date you finish each step in the margin. Milestone checks ask you to compare old and new recordings and texts.

## A note on Danish specifically

- **Sound is the problem, not grammar.** Danish grammar is among the simplest in Europe. Danish pronunciation and listening are among the hardest for English speakers because of the vowel inventory (over 20 vowel sounds), the stød (a glottal catch that distinguishes words), the soft d, and heavy reduction in fast speech. The guide front-loads sound work and never lets it drop.
- **Numbers are weird.** 50 is *halvtreds*, 70 is *halvfjerds*, 90 is *halvfems*. There is a dedicated step for this.
- **Half past** is said in relation to the *next* hour: *halv fire* is 3:30, not 4:30.
- **Literature and screen culture** are a stated goal of this programme. Every level includes a `Culture` track that builds familiarity with the Danish canon (H.C. Andersen, Kierkegaard, Blixen, Ditlevsen, Høeg and the living generation) and with the television and film that Danes actually reference (Matador, Olsen-banden, Borgen, Forbrydelsen, Klovn, the Dogme films). At A1 and A2 much of this is done in English so that, by B1, you already know what you are reading.
